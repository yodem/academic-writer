# Global editorial checks (venue-agnostic)

The editorial stage's **default checklist**. It is loaded on every run, whether or not a
venue profile exists, and a venue profile only ever *adds* to it — a venue can tighten a
check or name an extra one, never remove one from here.

These are general academic-publishing norms. They were distilled from a real
pre-submission rejection of a humanities article, but nothing here is specific to that
journal, that field or that language: every item below is a reason articles get returned
across the humanities. Treat them as the floor.

## The six checks

### 1. Object in focus (`genre-fit`)
Does the article take a **defined object** — one work, one argument, one corpus, one
named set — or does it survey a thinker's or field's output as a whole? A piece whose
stated aim is to trace and critique the foundations of an entire system is a book
proposal, not an article, and most venues will say so regardless of quality. Check the
article's own statement of aim against what it actually does.

### 2. Loaded terms used as premises (`term-undefined`)
Find every term doing **argumentative work** while going undefined — a contested value
word, a school of thought, a period label, an evaluative category. The failure is not
using such a term; it is resting a conclusion on it. Two shapes to catch:
- the term appears in the thesis but is never defined;
- the assumptions bound up in it would need a systematic discussion that would not fit in
  the article — which means the article must not depend on them.

### 3. Sweeping normative claims as starting points (`term-undefined` / `refocus`)
A claim that a whole outlook is simply mistaken, that one group reliably produces a better
outcome than another, or that a tradition's essence and purpose are X rather than Y, is a
**conclusion someone would have to argue**, not a premise. Flag it when it is asserted in
a sentence and then built upon. Ask specifically: are the criteria by which this judgement
is made stated anywhere?

### 4. Insider references (`insider-reference`)
Names, events, institutions or shorthand that need prior familiarity with a specific
social world, community or sub-field. The test is not whether the author's own readers
would follow it — it is whether **this venue's** readers can be expected to, and the
default answer for a general scholarly readership is no. This includes evaluative asides
about a community's internal norms, which read as opaque and as advocacy at once.

### 5. First-person and biographical material (`cut`)
The author's acquaintance with the subject, biographical episodes, personal reminiscence,
and epilogues about the author's relationship to the subject. These can be genuinely
significant to the author and still not be matter for the venue's readers. Flag the
pattern across a passage, not one stray "I".

### 6. Journalistic rather than research treatment (`refocus`)
A topic already argued at length in the press, or a live public controversy, carried into
an article without the framing that makes it research: an object of study, a method, a
position located in a literature. Also: sections that narrate or editorialize rather than
argue from evidence.

## A note on language, which is NOT this stage's job

A recurring reason articles are returned is pervasive language and syntax trouble —
recurring grammatical errors, non-standard phrasing, typos, syntax unsuited to academic
prose. Editors do not treat that as "a few technical corrections"; it can be a bar to
consideration on its own, independent of the argument's merit.

That work belongs to the **language** stage (`copyeditor`) and the **mechanical** stage
(`proofreader`), not here. The editorial stage should not raise it — but it is recorded in
this file because it explains why all three stages exist and why the mechanical stage's
recall matters as much as the argument's quality.

## How to use this with a venue profile

- **No venue profile:** evaluate against the six checks above, and say in `summary` that
  no venue was specified so only general norms were applied.
- **With a venue profile:** apply all six, then add anything the profile's own
  "what they reject" list names. Where the profile is stricter, follow the profile.
- **Never** invent a venue convention. If a check depends on knowing the venue's
  conventions and you do not, say so rather than guessing.
